# A-05 — Refusing to Compute Rather Than Guess

**Tags:** Reassessment · Engineering Validation

## The Question

The same real acquisition case was run twice, under two different frozen operating conditions, to see whether adding an explicit problem-diagnosis step before deeper analysis actually changed the quality of judgment — not just the appearance of it.

## What the system was given

An identical frozen participant package in both runs — one without a dedicated problem-diagnosis step first, one with it.

## What it found

Without the diagnosis step: inconsistencies in the sponsor's return arithmetic and value claims, and an insufficiently reconciled sponsor model. With the diagnosis step added first: the real controlling issue moved upstream, from a narrow model-reconciliation complaint to whether a critical capital-recycling and refinancing assumption in the investment thesis was actually supportable in the first place.

## The judgment / result

Both runs refused to execute a formal valuation model rather than invent unsupported inputs to produce one — treated as the strongest observed behavior, not a failure to answer. The diagnosis-first run additionally preserved competing causal explanations and identified what would break the thesis, before deciding what tools or models to even admit.

## What remained blocked, unresolved, or preserved

Even with the improvement, a meaningful gap to expert-level judgment remained open: deeper commercial questions about demand, product definition, and what operating evidence should be generated before committing more capital stayed underweighted. This is one same-case comparison, not proof of universal method effectiveness.

## What this case demonstrates

Refusing to produce a number when the evidence doesn't support one is treated as a commercially relevant behavior in its own right, not a shortcoming — and the system is tested for whether a method change improves judgment quality, not just whether it can generate a more polished-looking report.
