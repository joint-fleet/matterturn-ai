# Boeing and Hertz — Decomposing a Credit Event Into Its Real Causes

**Tags:** Evidence Boundary · Reassessment · Engineering Validation

## The Question

Over 18 months spanning 2019–2020, Boeing faced three genuinely different shocks in close succession — a company-specific safety and credit crisis, then a market-wide shock that hit everything at once, then a central-bank intervention — all visible in the price of the same bonds. Separately, when Hertz's operating company filed for Chapter 11 in 2020, did every one of its debt claims share the same fate? Can a single bond's price move, or a single company's bankruptcy, be split into its real, independent causes rather than collapsed into one story?

## What the system was given

Public filings and market data only: Boeing's SEC filings (the 737 MAX production halt, credit-rating actions), Treasury yield data, investment-grade credit-spread indices, and Federal Reserve facility announcements, alongside Boeing's own $25bn bond offering; and for Hertz, SEC filings, the Delaware Bankruptcy Court record, and the company's confirmed 2021 Plan of Reorganization.

## What it found

Boeing's bonds moved for at least three distinct, separately-dated reasons that do not reduce to one explanation: a 2019 issuer-specific credit deterioration with no accompanying rate move; a 2020 window where falling risk-free rates (a price tailwind) and market-wide liquidity stress (a price headwind) hit at the same time but pulled in opposite directions; and a Fed-intervention day where credit spreads narrowed while Treasury yields actually rose. Separately, Hertz's senior unsecured operating-company debt and its secured, vehicle-collateral-backed financing-subsidiary notes were genuinely different claims — the secured notes kept being serviced normally under forbearance throughout, while the unsecured claims were priced at deep distress.

## The judgment / result

The bond-price moves were held apart by cause rather than defaulted to "price fell, so credit must be worse." The two Hertz claim classes were tracked as separate propositions rather than one blended "the company defaulted" conclusion. The real, documented 2021 outcome diverged sharply from what pre-plan distressed pricing implied: every creditor class was paid in full, and the plan returned over $1bn of value to existing shareholders.

## What remained blocked, unresolved, or preserved

Seniority and capital-structure differentiation within Boeing's own debt was explicitly not attempted — the system found no genuine multi-tranche data to test, since Boeing's debt in this window was essentially all senior unsecured, and none was invented to fill the gap. Explicit guarantee-structure relationships between Hertz's subsidiaries were not modeled either, because sourcing the exact guarantor structure to a primary-document standard wasn't practical — the core finding (claim-specific, entity-specific recovery divergence) didn't require it, and the system said so rather than quietly assuming a structure it hadn't verified.

## What this case demonstrates

A single bond's price move can have more than one real, independent cause operating at once, and a single company's default does not mean uniform loss across every claim tied to it — recovery is claim-specific and entity-specific. The system can hold "the market priced in a bad outcome" separately from "the realized outcome was very different," without quietly assuming after the fact that the good outcome was foreseeable. This is engineering/research validation only — not investment advice, not a credit rating, and not a performance claim; the system explicitly disclaims being a duration calculator, DV01/convexity engine, or automated trading tool.
